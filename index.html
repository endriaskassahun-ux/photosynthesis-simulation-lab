Context and background

Plants make their own food by photosynthesis. Chlorophyll in the chloroplasts absorbs light energy. The plant uses this energy to turn carbon dioxide and water into glucose. Oxygen is released as a waste product. The plant stores extra glucose as starch.

Word equation: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (light energy, chlorophyll)
Symbol equation: 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2

Why count bubbles?

Elodea lives under water, so the oxygen it makes comes out of the stem as bubbles. More bubbles per minute means a faster rate of photosynthesis. The plant also uses some oxygen for respiration all the time. So in very dim light, the plant makes less oxygen than it uses, and no bubbles appear. Bubble counting is a quick method, but bubbles are not all the same size, so scientists sometimes collect and measure the gas volume instead.

What is a limiting factor?

A limiting factor is the factor in shortest supply. It holds the rate back. Example: at normal CO2 (420 ppm), making the lamp brighter speeds up photosynthesis only at first. Then the rate levels off, because now CO2 is limiting. If you add more CO2, the rate can rise higher again. Temperature works differently: the rate increases up to about 25 °C, then falls, because high temperatures damage the enzymes.

Why is green light used less?

Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light best and reflects more green light. That is why leaves look green. But leaves still absorb some green light and use it, so green light still drives photosynthesis, just more slowly.

How was the glucose estimated?

A bubble is about 0.6 mm³ of gas, but it is not pure oxygen. It also contains other gases from the water, such as nitrogen, and some oxygen dissolves before it forms a bubble. So we estimate each bubble carries about 0.2 mm³ of oxygen. For every 6 oxygen molecules released, the plant makes 1 glucose molecule. Each bubble contains billions of molecules, so 1 bubble does not equal 1 molecule. At standard conditions the sprig makes about 0.2 mg of glucose per hour, which matches published rates for a 10 cm sprig.
